Two Dollars a Search

I helped build a scraping fleet to beat Google’s CAPTCHAs. Two years later I deleted all of it — and the interesting part wasn’t the saving. It was how many of my own confident answers turned out to be wrong.

Rohan Sunwar · Darkmap, a cyber-threat-intelligence platform

Darkmap turns a search term into a ranked list of Telegram channels likely to be leaking data about it. Type AT&T, get back the channels trading its breached records. Police forces use it. It once contributed to solving a case.

Underneath, it worked by screen-scraping a Google Programmable Search Engine. And Google does not enjoy being screen-scraped: hit it repeatedly from one address and you get a CAPTCHA instead of results.

So the platform spread its requests across many IP addresses — six containerised services, eighteen tasks, each with its own public address, each behind its own load balancer. It worked. It cost $242 a month to serve 106 searches.

That is two dollars and five cents per search, for a product whose entire job is to answer a question in a text box.

The reframe

It wasn’t over-engineered. It was mis-sized.

The tempting story is that someone gold-plated the infrastructure. That’s not what happened, and getting this right mattered, because it changed what I was allowed to remove.

The CAPTCHA constraint was real. I measured it: a single IP address survives roughly ten to fifteen requests against this search engine before Google stops trusting it. And one user search isn’t one request — it’s twelve, because six different search queries run across two pages each.

So a single search nearly exhausts one address’s entire budget. Two searches from the same address and you’re blocked. The fleet wasn’t defensive over-building; it was the minimum that made a burst of twelve simultaneous requests survivable.

What had gone stale was the volume assumption. The architecture was sized for a product doing thousands of searches. It was serving three and a half a day. Nobody re-measured after launch, and a fixed cost sat there for two years quietly charging for capacity that never got used.

The bug wasn’t in the design. It was that nobody went back and checked whether the design’s assumptions still held.

The investigation

Five things I believed that measurement killed

I went in with a plan inherited from the previous handover: swap the scraping for Google’s official Custom Search JSON API, which serves the same search engine as structured JSON. Cheaper, faster, no browsers. It was written up as the single most important objective.

It was also the first thing to die.

01

“The official API will fix this” Killed

Two Google Cloud projects, both correctly configured — API enabled, valid keys, right project — both returning 403. I burned an hour on org policy, propagation lag, key restrictions and billing before reading the documentation properly:

Not recoverable by any account, any billing arrangement, any amount of clicking. The plan the whole project was organised around had been dead the entire time. Lesson: read the vendor’s deprecation notice before debugging your own configuration.

02

“Cheap proxies solve the IP problem” Killed

If the constraint is addresses, buy addresses. I tested two free proxy pools against the real search engine. One was half-burned on arrival. The other was flawless — 120 requests, zero CAPTCHAs — and for about twenty minutes I thought the problem was solved for six dollars a month.

Then I kept testing. After roughly 900 cumulative requests through those same ten addresses:

Shared datacentre addresses are a consumable, not an asset. And they don’t scale with concurrency: ten simultaneous users would mean 120 simultaneous requests, which at this rate needs ~120 addresses — more expensive than the fleet I was trying to delete.

03

“The fastest API wins” Killed

A search API returned results in 1.5 seconds against the incumbent’s 8. Cheaper too. I was ready to recommend it.

Then I diffed the actual channels against a baseline I’d captured from the live system, and found it reproduced 33 of 72. Worse, adding more result pages made the overlap flatten while unrelated channels piled up:

The missing channels weren’t deeper in the results. They weren’t there at all — because that API searches the open web, while Darkmap searches a curated search engine restricted to a handful of Telegram directory sites. Years of tuning live in that configuration.

That gave me the rule that sorted every remaining option: anything that renders our search engine preserves the results; anything that queries Google generally does not. A product manager would call it knowing where the moat is. I found it by accident, two hours in, because I diffed instead of trusting a latency number.

04

“A bigger plan will make it faster” Killed

The winning vendor renders our search engine server-side and handles addresses, fingerprinting and blocks internally. Search latency landed at 12–20 seconds — slower than the fleet’s 3. The obvious move was to pay for the higher tier.

I checked first. Higher tiers buy concurrency, and we had no queueing to remove: ten requests, ten workers, zero rate-limit errors across every test. The time was going into each individual request, which no plan tier changes. The upgrade would have bought one to two seconds for thirty dollars a month.

What did work was borrowed from distributed systems: since the twelve requests run in parallel, total latency equals the slowest one. So fire a duplicate for anything still running after seven seconds and take whichever answers first.

The median barely moved. The tail disappeared, and users tolerate a predictable wait far better than one that randomly doubles.

05

“I know what this system costs” Killed

After deleting the fleet I reported, confidently, that the account now had zero load balancers. I was wrong: the AWS CLI is scoped to one region at a time and I’d only queried the one everything lived in.

A forgotten load balancer in another region, created August 2024. Every target unhealthy. Its cluster: zero tasks. Serving nothing, for thirteen months, at $25.25 a month — roughly $300 already spent.

It never appeared in any cost review because every review had looked in one region. The handover document even recorded “the only six load balancers in the whole account.” That sentence was true of a region, not an account.

Product judgement

The two optimisations I turned down

Both were mine, both were technically sound, and both were wrong for this product. Deciding that is the part of the job that isn’t engineering.

Caching Rejected

Repeat searches would drop from twelve seconds to under one, free, on infrastructure already running. Obvious win — except the results are sorted by recency, and this is threat intelligence. A cached hit is stale by construction. “Here’s what was leaking two hours ago” is a different product from the one people rely on. The user made this call and was right.

Moving the AI ranking off the critical path Rejected

Six of the twelve seconds is an AI pass that scores and re-sorts channels by relevance. Return the list unranked, re-sort when scores arrive, save six seconds of perceived wait. I proposed it before tracing what the client actually does with the response:

The client immediately fetches message history for the ranked top ten. Return an unranked list and it fetches the wrong ten, then visibly reshuffles under the user. The optimisation didn’t save six seconds; it moved the cost somewhere more expensive and less honest.

Hedging survived the same test: duplicate requests hit the identical URL, so the merged result set and everything downstream are untouched. Only the waiting changes.

The trade

Why 12 seconds beat 3

On paper I made the product four times slower. In practice I made it available, and that reframing came from one query rather than an opinion.

The fleet had an idle-shutdown that scaled it to zero after thirty minutes of quiet — sensible, given the cost. But nothing in the codebase started it again. I checked the backend, both client applications, their built bundles. Nothing. Starting it required someone to call an endpoint by hand.

At three and a half searches a day, cold was the normal state. The honest comparison was never 3s versus 12s:

Twelve seconds that always works is a better product than three seconds that needs someone to remember to boot a fleet. And the thing that made that trade acceptable was verifying result quality first, not the latency: of the security-relevant channels a real analyst cares about — the breach forums, the leak brokers — the replacement returned 13 of 13. The divergence was entirely in exam-prep and news channels the AI already scores near zero.

Outcome

What actually changed

Deleted along the way: six services, eighteen tasks, seven load balancers across two regions, thirty-nine unused container repositories, an idle-shutdown function, a scheduler, three alarms, and a browser automation stack. The replacement is one function with no dependencies outside the standard library.

Also deleted: a public, unauthenticated endpoint that spun up eighteen billable containers on request. Something external was still calling it — twice on the day I found it. Not the product; neither client referenced it. Someone’s saved script, most likely, quietly restarting the fleet an idle-shutdown had just switched off.

What I’d carry forward

Five things worth keeping

  • Capture the baseline before you touch anything. Every good decision here traced back to one artefact: a recorded snapshot of what the live system returned for a set of queries. Without it “the results look fine” is a vibe. With it, it’s a diff.
  • Distinguish the constraint from the assumption. The CAPTCHA limit was real and had to be honoured. The traffic estimate was stale and could be dropped. Conflating them either paralyses you or breaks the product.
  • The fastest option is not the option. A 1.5-second API that loses half your results is not a faster product, it’s a worse one. Latency is the easiest thing to measure, which is exactly why it’s the easiest to over-weight.
  • Trace the consumer before optimising the producer. The async-ranking idea died the moment I read what the client did with the response. Two minutes of reading would have saved proposing it.
  • Check whether the thing you’re protecting is switched on. Discovering the fleet was cold most of the time didn’t change one number — it inverted which option was better.

There’s a version of this write-up where I had a hypothesis, tested it, and shipped a 92% saving. That version is tidier and less useful. What actually happened is that five confident answers — four of them mine — got killed by measurement, and the migration was decided by a diff nobody had thought to run.

The saving is the outcome. The diff was the work.
